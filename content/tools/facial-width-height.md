## What is fWHR (facial width-to-height ratio)?

**fWHR**, or **facial width-to-height ratio**, compares how wide your face is with how tall your upper face is:

> **fWHR = cheekbone width ÷ upper face height**

Cheekbone width (also called bizygomatic width) is the distance between the widest points of your cheekbones. Upper face height is the distance from your brows to your upper lip. A higher fWHR means a relatively wide, short face; a lower fWHR means a narrower, longer one.

fWHR has become one of the most talked-about facial measurements because psychologists have spent the last two decades studying how it relates to how people are perceived — especially how dominant, aggressive or trustworthy a face looks. In looksmaxxing communities, a high fWHR is often seen as a marker of a masculine, "dominant" face.

## How the fWHR calculator works

The calculator runs Google's MediaPipe Face Mesh in your browser — your photo isn't uploaded — and places 478 landmarks on your face. It measures:

- **Cheekbone width** — between the left and right cheekbone landmarks at the widest part of your face.
- **Upper face height** — from the average height of your brows down to the top of your upper lip.

Your fWHR is the first divided by the second. The calculator also shows a second ratio — cheekbone width compared with your full face height from forehead to chin — for context.

| fWHR | Result |
|---|---|

The score out of 100 is highest around an fWHR of 2.0 and falls the further you move from it in either direction, so both very wide and very narrow faces score lower.

## What research says about fWHR

- **Perceived dominance and aggression.** In a widely cited 2008 study, Justin Carré and Cheryl McCormick found that men with higher fWHR behaved more aggressively in a lab task, and that university and professional ice hockey players with wider faces spent more time in the penalty box. A 2015 meta-analysis by Shawn Geniole and colleagues concluded that fWHR is linked to both actual and perceived aggression, though the effects are small.
- **Trust.** A 2010 study by Michael Stirrat and David Perrett found that people trusted men with wider faces less in an economic game — and that wider-faced men were more likely to exploit that trust.
- **Leadership.** A 2011 study by Elaine Wong and colleagues reported that companies led by CEOs with wider faces achieved better financial performance on average.
- **Is it really a male trait?** It's debated. A 2007 study by Weston, Friday and Liò found that fWHR differs between men and women, but a 2012 study by Robin Kramer and colleagues found no reliable sex difference in white European faces. fWHR is probably less of a "masculinity score" than online discussions suggest.

The key takeaway: fWHR is a real, measurable proportion that influences first impressions, but the effects found in research are small and averaged across many people. It doesn't determine anyone's personality.

## Is a high fWHR attractive?

It depends on the face and who's looking. A high fWHR gives a broad, strong look that many associate with masculinity, which is why it's prized in looksmaxxing circles. But very wide faces can also read as less approachable, and attractiveness research generally finds that balanced, typical proportions do well. That's why this calculator scores the middle of the range — around 2.0 — highest, rather than rewarding the widest faces.

## Tips for an accurate fWHR measurement

fWHR is sensitive to head angle and camera distance:

1. **Keep your head level.** Tilting your chin down shortens your upper face in the photo and inflates your fWHR; tilting up does the opposite.
2. **Relax your brows.** Raising them lengthens the upper face and lowers your fWHR.
3. **Keep your lips closed and neutral.** Smiling moves your upper lip.
4. **Take the photo from at least arm's length.** Close-up selfies exaggerate the centre of the face and distort width.

## Can you change your fWHR?

Your fWHR is set by your cheekbones and the vertical structure of your midface, so it doesn't change much. Body fat can change how wide your face looks, and facial hair changes the lower face but not the brow-to-lip height this ratio uses. Surgical options for the cheekbones or midface exist but are major procedures and not something to pursue for a single ratio.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is fWHR?

fWHR stands for facial width-to-height ratio: the width of your face at the cheekbones divided by the height of your upper face, from brows to upper lip. Higher values mean a wider, shorter face.

### How do you calculate facial width-to-height ratio?

Measure the distance between the widest points of your cheekbones, then the distance from your brows to your upper lip, and divide the first by the second. This calculator measures both from a photo automatically.

### What is a good fWHR?

There's no single "good" value, but this calculator classes 1.8–2.2 as balanced and scores around 2.0 highest. Above 2.2 is a wide face, often described as dominant.

### What is a high fWHR?

On this calculator, an fWHR above 2.2 is high. High-fWHR faces are broad relative to their upper face height and are often perceived as more dominant.

### Does fWHR predict aggression?

Research has found a small link between higher fWHR and both actual and perceived aggression, mostly in men. The effect is weak and averaged across many people, so it says very little about any individual.

### Is fWHR only for men?

fWHR can be measured on anyone. Research disagrees on whether it really differs between men and women, and much of the research on dominance and aggression focuses on men.
