## What are facial thirds?

**Facial thirds** divide the face vertically into three sections:

- **Upper third** — from the hairline to the brows.
- **Middle third** — from the brows to the base of the nose.
- **Lower third** — from the base of the nose to the bottom of the chin.

The classical rule of facial proportion says that in a well-balanced face, these three sections are **roughly equal in height** — each about a third of the face. The idea goes back to Renaissance artists, who used it as a guide for drawing faces, and it's still used today by portrait artists, orthodontists and cosmetic surgeons as a quick check on vertical balance.

This free **facial thirds calculator** measures your three thirds from a photo and shows exactly what percentage of your face each one takes up.

## How to measure facial thirds

You can measure facial thirds by hand with a ruler on a straight-on photo: mark your hairline, the line between your brows, the base of your nose and the bottom of your chin, then compare the three distances. The calculator does the same thing more precisely:

1. It runs Google's MediaPipe Face Mesh in your browser (your photo isn't uploaded) and places 478 landmarks on your face.
2. It finds four reference heights: the top of your forehead, the average height of your two brows, the base of your nose and the bottom of your chin.
3. It measures the upper, middle and lower thirds between them and expresses each one as a **percentage of total face height**.
4. It compares each percentage with the ideal 33.3% and turns the average difference into a score out of 100.

One difference from the hand-measured version: hairlines vary a lot and are often hidden by hair, so the calculator measures the upper third from the top of the forehead area the model detects, which usually sits a little below the hairline. If your upper third reads slightly short, that's part of the reason — the middle and lower thirds are the more reliable comparison.

As a rough guide, each percentage point an average third is away from 33.3% costs five points of score. Look at your three percentages to see where the difference is:

- **A long upper third** often comes with a high or receding hairline. A fringe or textured hairstyle can visually shorten it.
- **A long middle third** means a longer nose and midface — the [midface ratio calculator](/tools/midface-ratio) looks at this in more detail.
- **A long lower third** is common in men and usually comes from a longer chin or jaw. A short lower third can make the face look compressed. The [lower third analyzer](/tools/lower-third) breaks it down into philtrum, lips and chin.

## Do real faces have equal thirds?

Mostly not. When the anthropometrist Leslie Farkas measured the faces of young adults against the classical canons, he found that the classical canons — equal thirds among them — describe very few people exactly. Most attractive faces are close to equal thirds rather than precisely equal.

In general, the lower third tends to be slightly longer in men than in women, and the upper third varies a lot with hairline. So equal thirds are best treated as a guide to **balance**, not a target to hit to the decimal place. A face where no third is dramatically longer than the others reads as harmonious.

## Facial thirds vs the golden ratio and fifths

- **Facial thirds** check vertical balance with three equal sections.
- **Facial fifths** check horizontal balance: across the eyes, the face should be about five eye-widths wide. The [eye spacing calculator](/tools/eye-spacing) measures this.
- **The golden ratio** compares features in a 1.618 proportion rather than equal parts. See the [golden ratio face calculator](/tools/golden-ratio).

## Tips for an accurate facial thirds measurement

1. **Keep your head perfectly level.** Tilting your chin up shortens the lower third in the photo; tilting it down lengthens it.
2. **Pull your hair back** so your forehead is visible.
3. **Relax your face.** Raised brows lengthen the middle third, and an open mouth lengthens the lower third.
4. **Take the photo from arm's length or further**, at eye level rather than from above or below.

## Can you change your facial thirds?

The underlying proportions are set by your skull and jaw, but how they look can change:

- **Hairstyles** change the visible upper third — fringes shorten it, slicked-back styles lengthen it.
- **Beards** change how long the lower third looks.
- **Weight loss** can make the lower third look longer and more defined.
- **Medical options** like hairline lowering, chin surgery and jaw surgery exist for structural changes, but they're major decisions to discuss with a qualified surgeon.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are facial thirds?

Facial thirds divide the face into three vertical sections: hairline to brows (upper third), brows to the base of the nose (middle third) and the base of the nose to the chin (lower third). In a balanced face, each is roughly a third of the face's height.

### How do you measure facial thirds?

Mark the hairline, the line between the brows, the base of the nose and the bottom of the chin on a straight-on photo, then compare the three distances. This calculator does it automatically and shows each third as a percentage of your face.

### What are balanced facial thirds?

Balanced facial thirds are close to 33% each. On this calculator, a score of 88 or above means all three are within a few percentage points of equal.

### Do I have equal facial thirds?

Upload a straight-on photo to find out. Most people don't have perfectly equal thirds — it's normal for one third to be a few percent longer than the others.

### Which facial third is usually the longest?

It varies. In many men the lower third is slightly longer, and people with high or receding hairlines often have a longer upper third.

### How can I get equal facial thirds?

You can't change the bone structure without surgery, but hairstyles, fringes and beards can visually rebalance your thirds. Surgical options exist for significant imbalances and should be discussed with a qualified surgeon.
